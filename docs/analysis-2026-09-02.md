# The capture session, analysed — 2026-09-02

This is the analysis pass over everything captured overnight on 2026-09-02. The raw material and
the conditions it was shot under are in [measured-2026-09-02.md](measured-2026-09-02.md); that file
records what was *captured*, this one records what it *says*. The tables it produced are in
`tools/calibration/derived/`, with a data dictionary in
[derived/README.md](../tools/calibration/derived/README.md).

## What this whole exercise is for

Stated plainly, because the point of the capture session was to make this possible rather than to
collect files: **the goal is to be able to model and simulate these strips well enough that the app
can be developed and reasoned about without them in the room.** Every behaviour that surprises
someone watching the real strip — a fade that is not smooth, two strips out of step, a colour that
does not look like the one that was asked for, a dim setting that does nothing — has to be
reproducible from a description of the device rather than discovered by plugging one in. So the
data has to cover both halves of the path: **what the strip does with a command that reaches it**
(the light), and **which commands reach it at all** (the wire). Both halves were captured. Both are
now fitted.

A model is only as good as its provenance, so every number below says how far it can be trusted,
and `derived/device_model.json` carries that verdict per parameter in machine-readable form.

## 1. The strip's response to a colour byte is violently compressive

This is the largest single result and it changes how the app should think about colour.

| commanded byte | light, as a fraction of byte 255 |
|---|---|
| 0, 1 | 0 — nothing at all |
| 2 | 0.052 |
| 4 | 0.113 |
| 8 | 0.177 |
| 16 | 0.303 |
| 32 | 0.454 |
| 64 | 0.633 |
| 128 | 0.810 |
| 192 | 0.894 |
| 255 | 1.000 |

Half the strip's total light output is reached by **byte 40**. The top half of the byte range —
128 through 255 — buys the last 19%. A power law fits it to within 0.07 across the range:

```
light ≈ (byte / 255) ** 0.416
```

**Three consequences.**

- Any code that treats the colour byte as proportional to light is wrong by up to a factor of
  three in the mid range. A linear cross-fade between two colours does not pass through the
  midpoint; it passes well above it, which is why fades on this hardware look front-loaded.
- **The strip cannot be dimmed below about 5% of full output using colour bytes.** Byte 1 is black
  and byte 2 is already at 5%. There is no gentler step available. Deep dimming has to go through
  the firmware brightness setting, and the good news is that setting was separately shown not to
  flicker (§6) — so headroom scaling has both the mechanism it needs and the evidence it is safe.
- A perceptual ramp needs the *inverse* of this curve, not a gamma curve. Sending bytes on a
  straight line produces a light curve that is already near-flat by the middle.

**How far to trust it.** The ramp was shot at two exposures because no single one holds byte 1 and
byte 255. Between bytes 48 and 203 the two exposures agree to within 5% after a single scale
factor, which is exactly what a change of exposure should be in linear light — that agreement is
what makes this a measurement rather than a picture of a camera. Below byte 48 they disagree by up
to 42%. Both takes agree on the *shape* — strongly compressive, with a floor at byte 2 far above
zero — but the exact fraction at, say, byte 8 is somewhere between 0.18 and 0.30 and this data
cannot narrow it further. `response_stitched.csv` carries both takes and the disagreement per row
rather than presenting a single confident number.

## 2. The write ceiling is 15 Hz per device, and the app is what sets it

The previous reading of the wire logs was that "roughly half of all writes never reach the strip,
and it depends on rate". That is true but it is the symptom. The rate stairs say what is actually
happening:

| requested | offered to the queue | delivered | survived |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 Hz | 1.9 | 1.9 | 100% |
| 10 Hz | 7.9 | 8.1 | 100% |
| 20 Hz | 15.8 | 14.9 | 94% |
| 30 Hz | 23.9 | 14.6 | 61% |
| 50 Hz | 38.6 | 15.0 | 39% |
| 100 Hz | 72.2 | 14.9 | 21% |

**Delivered rate is pinned at 15 Hz from 20 Hz upward and never moves.** The median gap between
acks is 59–60 ms at every stair above the knee, with a floor of 54 ms. So the model is not "half
the writes die"; it is:

```
delivered_rate = min(requested_rate, 15 Hz)
acked_fraction = delivered_rate / requested_rate
```

The cause is in the app, not the radio and not the strip. `DeviceWriteManager` paces writes by
`currentPacingMs`, and the transport supplies a default of 50 ms per device
(`RgbControllerViewModel.kt:864`). Fifty milliseconds of pacing plus about nine of write turnaround
is the 59 ms cadence, and 59 ms is 17 Hz. Everything offered above that is coalesced away by
`updateCommand`, which drops any queued command of the same type byte — correctly, and by design.

Two things follow, and the second matters more than the first.

- The uneven red/blue alternation Joe watched in `spacing_staircase` is the staircase climbing past
  15 Hz. It is app-side pacing, exactly as suspected, and it is a configured value rather than a
  fault.
- **The strip's actual speed limit has never been measured.** Every fast sequence was throttled to
  15 Hz before it reached the hardware, so nothing in this session says what the strip could do at
  30 or 60 Hz. The `sustained_load` figure of 407,282 writes at a 2 ms median gap is offered writes
  — that run predates the wire log and has no ack side at all. Do not quote it as throughput.

## 3. A second strip costs nothing — now with a number on it

One device against two, same sequence back to back: **each device gets its own full 15 Hz**, and
the per-stair delivered rates for the two devices in the two-device run are identical to within one
ack. There is no shared budget to model and `multiDeviceThroughputFactor` describes nothing real.

## 4. Wire-to-light latency is about 40 ms, not 57

Two `latency_camera` runs exist. **One of them is unusable**: its exposure was still hunting, so the
light level sagged by 49% during every on-period and each threshold crossing was measured against a
moving target. The other run holds its level to within 5% and is the one to quote.

| edge | n | median | mean | sd | min | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rise (black → white) | 20 | 39.7 ms | 43.3 | 25.2 | 12.0 | 127.4 |
| fall (white → black) | 20 | 36.1 ms | 56.5 | 46.6 | 7.6 | 170.3 |

These are lower than the 57 ms / 53 ms quoted during capture, and the difference is method rather
than measurement. At 30 fps all a recording can say is "dark at this frame, lit by the next".
Taking the frame where the light was first seen makes every edge late by up to a full frame
interval; taking the midpoint of the bracket does not, and 39.7 + half a frame is 56. Each edge is
stored in `latency_edges.csv` as its bracket, so the uncertainty stays attached to the number.

Rise and fall still agree closely: **the strip is not slower to light than to darken.** The spread
is far wider than the 33 ms sampling grid, so what is visible is real delivery jitter.

## 5. Colour transitions are steps, and the channels do not interfere

- **Every one of 18 hard colour jumps completed inside a single frame interval.** The firmware does
  not glide between colours, so a model needs no slew rate — a commanded change is instantaneous to
  within 33 ms, which is finer than the delivery jitter above it.
- **Driving one channel does not drag the others in a level-dependent way.** The camera sees light
  in all three channels whatever is driven — its filters overlap and the emitters are not narrow —
  but the share held by each channel stays constant to within 4% as the driven channel is swept
  from byte 32 to 255. Constant share means the camera; a share that moved with level would have
  meant the strip. **R, G and B can be modelled independently.**

## 6. Deep dimming does not flicker — reproduced from the raw frames

| duty | frames held | mean luma | sd | cv |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 585 | 5.69 | 0.112 | 2.0% |
| 10% | 582 | 17.53 | 0.123 | 0.7% |
| 40% | 584 | 53.42 | 0.281 | 0.5% |
| 100% | 593 | 82.58 | 0.425 | 0.5% |

Every standard deviation is a fraction of one 8-bit level at a pinned 5 ms exposure, and the
absolute spread is *smallest* at 5% — the opposite of what a visible carrier would do. This
reproduces the figures read off during capture to three significant figures, which is the check
that the frame-trimming used here is picking the same held region a person would.

Separately, the firmware brightness ramp shows that **1% and 2% emit identically** — the setting has
no effect below 3%.

## 7. What the per-LED macro take can and cannot say

The macro take resolves seven individual emitters. It says one useful thing cleanly: **every LED
cuts in at the same byte** — all dark at byte 1, all lit at byte 2 — so there is no per-emitter
threshold to model.

It cannot carry a per-LED response *curve*, because the take is clipping. Summed over all seven
LEDs and normalised to byte 32, it reads much flatter at the bottom than the curve in §1 — which
was measured at the same exposure convention, on the same ramp, only on the wide framing:

| byte | macro take | §1 curve |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.256 | 0.114 |
| 4 | 0.352 | 0.249 |
| 8 | 0.481 | 0.390 |
| 16 | 0.694 | 0.668 |

A curve that sags toward the top is a measurement whose top is saturating: on the telephoto the
emitter cores fill enough pixels to clip before the cell average can see them. So the 14–33% raw
spread between LEDs mixes real emitter differences with per-LED clipping and lens geometry, and
nothing here can separate them. Answering per-LED uniformity properly needs an exposure chosen for
that framing rather than inherited from the wide one.

## 8. The CCT sweep produced no light whatsoever

All 25 steps of `cct_sweep` — warm 0–100% crossed with cold 0–100% — measure **exactly zero**, and
not only inside the LED mask: the whole-frame mean is zero for the entire family, while the steps
either side of it register normally. The sequence ran, the writes went out, and the strip emitted
nothing.

This is a hard null, and it is either that the `Fireworks` unit has no white channel or that the
CCT command is not the one this firmware wants. It is a protocol question, not a re-shoot: no
amount of filming will change a strip that is dark.

## What is missing

Asked for explicitly. In the order worth doing.

1. **Mode Capture has still never been run.** Unchanged from the last handoff and still the biggest
   genuinely-unblocked item: roughly 200 built-in modes carry guessed names, categories and
   directions. It is UI-driven rather than an adb sequence, and its own line-sampling has never
   been verified once.
2. **The strip's real rate ceiling is unknown.** Everything fast in this session was throttled to
   15 Hz by the app's own pacing before it reached the hardware. One `rate_ramp` with pacing set to
   0 or 10 ms would say whether the strip can take 30 or 60 Hz — and that answer decides whether the
   visualiser has headroom or is already at the wall. This is a small run and it is the cheapest
   large thing left.
3. **The bottom of the response curve is not settled.** Bytes below 48 are the region the app cares
   about most for dimming, and the two exposures disagree by up to 42% there. A third take at an
   intermediate exposure — or a RAW capture, which removes the camera's tone curve from the argument
   entirely — would close it.
4. **The CCT null needs a protocol answer**, per §8.
5. **`sustained_load` has no wire log.** Its 407k writes are offered, not delivered; endurance at
   the *delivered* rate has not been measured. Re-running it now that the wire log exists is cheap.
6. **Per-LED uniformity needs its own exposure**, per §7.
7. **`docs/positions.md` still assumes the long vertical strand is consecutive LEDs on the wire.**
   Unverified, and every Mode Capture result rests on it. Running a mode with an obvious chase and
   watching whether the movement travels smoothly along the strand settles it in a minute.
8. **No absolute photometry.** Everything here is relative, because nothing in frame had a known
   output. That was the right trade and nothing asked of the data needed otherwise — but if an
   absolute figure is ever wanted, it needs a reference in shot, not a re-analysis.
9. **The PWM smear at 5–10% was skipped** because the strip's cable would not reach far enough to
   sweep the camera. The stationary `pwm_probe` answered the flicker question it existed to ask, so
   this is a loose end rather than a gap.

## Corrections to what was written during capture

- "Roughly half of all writes never reach the strip" is the symptom of a 15 Hz ceiling, not a
  property of the wire. Below 15 Hz nothing is lost at all. See §2.
- The 57 ms / 53 ms latency figures were crossing-frame and biased high by half a frame interval.
  See §4.
- `sustained_load`'s 407,282 writes are offered writes, not delivered ones. See §2.
- One of the two `latency_camera` runs is not usable. See §4.
- The concern recorded during capture was that `dark_ramp` might have one clipped point at byte 32.
  On the wide framing nothing clips until well above that — the dark `full_ramp` take is good to
  byte 203. On the **macro** framing the clipping starts far lower and affects the whole take. See
  §7.
